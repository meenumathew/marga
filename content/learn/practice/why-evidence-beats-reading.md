# Why Evidence Beats Reading

Reading a note feels like progress. It rarely is. You can finish fifty notes and still freeze the moment you have to build the thing. Mastery lives in the gap between recognising an idea and being able to use it, and only one activity closes that gap: producing work and getting feedback on it.

## Consumption is not competence

Marking a note read records that you showed up. It does not record that you can apply the idea. A tracker that counts notes read measures attendance. A tracker that counts evidence measures skill.

That is why this site separates two things:

- **Read all of a section** means you have been through the material.
- **Mastered a section** means you read it _and_ produced at least one piece of evidence in it.

The second one is the claim worth making.

## What counts as evidence

Evidence is any concrete output that exists after you close the note:

- a build or a feature that runs;
- a refactor with a before and after;
- a written trade-off, design note, or explanation;
- a talk, walkthrough, or teach-back;
- feedback you received from someone else.

Each one forces you to convert a passive idea into an active decision. That conversion is the learning.

## Feedback is the accelerant

Working alone hides your blind spots. Someone further along sees in a minute what would take you a month to notice, so feedback from other people is the fastest way to correct course. It shortens the loop between doing something and finding out whether it was any good.

So feedback is not a footnote here. It is a first-class kind of evidence. When a reviewer, mentor, or teammate tells you something useful, log it with their name attached. Over time the feedback log becomes a map of your real weak spots.

## The loop to repeat

1. Read the smallest note that helps the work in front of you.
2. Produce one output from it.
3. Show that output to someone and capture their feedback.
4. Log both on the [Evidence](/evidence) page.
5. Watch section mastery and the scorecard move, because they now reflect what you can do.

If a note never leads to an output, it was reference, not learning. Close it and move on.

## Where the idea comes from

You do not need any of this to use the system, but it is worth knowing the model is not invented here.

The research behind deliberate practice (Anders Ericsson) found that hours alone predict very little. What separates people who improve from people who plateau is practising at the edge of their current ability, on a specific weakness, with feedback that tells them whether it worked. The popular "10,000 hours" version of this drops the part that does the work; Ericsson spent years correcting it. Time is the cost, not the mechanism.

That is the whole reason this site refuses to reward notes marked read. A read count measures hours. An evidence log measures attempts at the edge, and the feedback kind records the correction. If you want the same argument told through the lives of people who did it, Robert Greene's _Mastery_ traces the long apprenticeship pattern, and the loop above is a small, mechanical version of it.
